How Maryland Fits Into America’s Loneliness Problem

A new interactive atlas tracks how Americans disconnect — from friends, community organizations, religious life and one another — across states and over time.

The Atlas

Loneliness is often described as a private feeling. The Social Disconnection Atlas treats it differently: as part of a broader pattern of weakening social connection.

The atlas maps multiple indicators of social disconnection across the United States throughout 2024. Users can compare states, switch between indicators and examine what the project calls a “disconnection fingerprint” — a profile showing how different forms of social withdrawal appear within a state.

The indicators go beyond loneliness alone. They include:

  • infrequent religious participation,
  • limited involvement in clubs or organizations,
  • reduced contact with friends and relatives,
  • inconsistent emotional support,
  • infrequent phone or text communication,
  • and self-reported loneliness.

Together, these measures attempt to capture something larger than individual isolation: whether the routines and institutions that sustain connection are becoming weaker, less dependable or unevenly distributed.

How to explore the atlas

Choose an indicator, move through the 2024 timeline and click states to compare trends and disconnection fingerprints across the country. Hover over charts for exact estimates, then open the full atlas to explore your own state.

The atlas opens with a choropleth map of the United States. Darker shades indicate higher levels of disconnection for a selected metric. Changing the indicator can dramatically reshape the map: a state that reports relatively low loneliness may still show weaker community participation or lower emotional support.

A timeline slider reveals how these measures shift across the year. Selecting a state opens two additional views: a trend chart comparing that state with the national average, and a “disconnection fingerprint,” a radar-style visualization that compares all indicators simultaneously.

Maryland

Maryland is often associated with prosperity: high household incomes, dense suburbs, elite universities, federal employment and proximity to political power. Yet the atlas suggests that economic stability does not necessarily guarantee social connection.

In the Social Disconnection Atlas’s most recent reporting period — Aug. 20 to Sept. 16, 2024 — roughly 9.9 percent of Maryland adults reported usually or always feeling lonely, below the national estimate of 13 percent.

At first glance, Maryland appears comparatively well-positioned. But the atlas reveals a more complicated pattern.

The state aligns more closely with national averages on measures related to organizational participation, emotional support and social contact. The result is a mixed social profile rather than a simple story of isolation or resilience. Maryland performs better than the national average on some dimensions of disconnection while remaining vulnerable on others.

The atlas also challenges the assumption that loneliness is concentrated primarily in economically struggling places. Social disconnection appears in affluent suburbs, professional corridors and highly educated communities as well.

The trend chart highlights another important feature of the data: disconnection changes over time. Earlier in 2024, Maryland tracked closer to the national average before diverging again later in the summer.

The atlas’s “disconnection fingerprint” compares multiple forms of social withdrawal at once. Instead of ranking states with a single score, it reveals how different dimensions of connection weaken unevenly.

Maryland’s profile suggests relatively lower loneliness than the national average while remaining closer to national patterns in areas like organizational participation and emotional support. Other states may show the opposite pattern: stronger social participation but higher loneliness, or weaker communication despite stronger community involvement.

The fingerprint makes these combinations visible.

What’s at stake?

Researchers have increasingly linked social isolation to physical and mental health risks, including depression, anxiety, cardiovascular disease and reduced life expectancy.

Communities with weaker social ties also often experience lower institutional trust, reduced civic participation and diminished resilience during crises.

The atlas does not argue that Americans have stopped connecting altogether. Instead, it suggests something quieter: that many of the structures once responsible for sustaining connection — religious institutions, civic organizations, friendships and informal routines — may be becoming less stable, less visible or harder to maintain.

By mapping these patterns geographically, the atlas makes it possible to compare not only where loneliness exists, but how different forms of disconnection emerge differently across the country.

For Maryland, the picture is neither catastrophic nor reassuringly simple. The state appears less lonely than the nation overall in recent data, yet still reflects broader patterns of social withdrawal visible throughout the atlas.

The larger question is not whether Americans feel lonely everywhere in the same way.

It is whether the forms of connection that once held communities together are changing — and where those changes are becoming most visible.
